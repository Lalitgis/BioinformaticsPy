Why align sequences at all?

Alignment lines up two (or more) sequences so that similar/homologous positions sit in the same column — even when insertions or deletions have shifted things out of sync. It's the foundation for measuring similarity, finding conserved functional regions, and building evolutionary trees.

When breeders compare a resistant vs. susceptible crop variety's version of a resistance gene, alignment is exactly how they spot the handful of causal mutations among thousands of identical bases.

Global vs. local alignment

TypeAlgorithmAlignsBest for
GlobalNeedleman-Wunschthe entire length of both sequences, end to end two sequences of similar length/full homology (e.g., two full orthologous genes)
LocalSmith-Watermanonly the best-matching sub-region finding a short motif/domain inside a much longer sequence, or very divergent sequences
from Bio import Align
aligner = Align.PairwiseAligner()
aligner.mode = "global"   # or "local"

Scoring: match/mismatch/gap penalties

aligner = Align.PairwiseAligner()
aligner.mode = "global"
aligner.match_score = 2
aligner.mismatch_score = -1
aligner.open_gap_score = -2
aligner.extend_gap_score = -0.5

alignments = aligner.align(seq1, seq2)
best = alignments[0]
print(best)
print("Score:", best.score)
Gap opening is penalized more heavily than gap extension — biologically, one 5-base deletion event is far more plausible than five independent single-base deletions, so we want alignments to prefer fewer, longer gaps over many short ones.

Percent identity — a simple, widely reported metric

def percent_identity(alignment):
    aligned_seq1, aligned_seq2 = alignment[0], alignment[1]
    matches = sum(a == b for a, b in zip(str(aligned_seq1), str(aligned_seq2)) if a != "-" and b != "-")
    aligned_len = len(str(aligned_seq1))
    return matches / aligned_len * 100
